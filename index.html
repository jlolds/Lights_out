Shutdown 101

What Is a Government Shutdown?

The federal government's year starts on October 1. Before then, Congress is supposed to pass the bills that give every agency its money for the year. If it can't agree, it can pass a short-term patch called a continuing resolution that keeps money flowing at last year's levels.

If Congress passes neither, and the President doesn't sign one into law, the money simply runs out. Agencies without money must stop most of their work. That is a shutdown. It is not a choice the agency makes. It is what the law requires.

Shutdowns are usually about a political fight, not about the agencies themselves. NIH can be shut down over an argument that has nothing to do with medical research. The longest shutdown so far, in fall 2025, lasted 43 days.

The Antideficiency Act

This law, first passed in 1870, is why shutdowns happen the way they do. It says three simple things:

  1. No money, no spending. A federal official may not spend or promise to spend money Congress hasn't provided. Signing a contract, ordering supplies, or keeping an employee on the job all count as spending.
  2. No free work. The government may not accept volunteer labor from its employees. This stops agencies from pressuring workers to "volunteer" and then sending Congress the bill later.
  3. One exception: emergencies. Work may continue if it is needed to protect human life or government property from real, near-term harm.

Breaking the law is serious. Officials can be suspended or fired. Knowingly and willfully violating it can bring a fine or even prison time. In practice, agency lawyers read the exception narrowly.

Who Works and Who Goes Home

CategoryWhat It MeansNIH Examples
ExceptedProtects life or property. Keeps working, but is not paid until the shutdown ends.Nurses caring for patients already in the hospital, NIH police, building engineers
FurloughedSent home. May not work, not even check email. Not paid.Most scientists, lab technicians, office staff, press staff

Every agency writes a shutdown plan ahead of time listing who is excepted. Furloughed workers have usually gotten back pay once the shutdown ends, but they don't know that for sure while it's happening, and their rent is still due.

What This Means at NIH

  • Most of NIH's money goes out as grants to universities. About a tenth stays home for NIH's own labs and hospital. That in-house part is called the intramural program. This game is about the intramural program.
  • The NIH Clinical Center in Bethesda is a hospital built entirely for research. Patients come from all over the country to try experimental treatments, often after everything else has failed.
  • In a shutdown, patients already in the hospital keep getting care. That's protecting life. But new patients usually aren't admitted to trials. In the 2013 shutdown, NIH's director told reporters the Clinical Center was turning away new patients every week, including children with cancer.
  • Lab experiments can't just be paused. Cells die, mouse studies that take years are ruined, and freezers full of samples need someone watching them.

The People

Everyone in this game is fictional. They are based on the kinds of people who are actually caught in a shutdown.

Ellie Kowalski, age 9

Ellie is a fourth-grader from Toledo, Ohio. She loves horses and soccer. Last year she was diagnosed with a rare childhood cancer. Two rounds of standard treatment at her hometown hospital didn't work.

Her doctors found a new experimental treatment being tested at the NIH Clinical Center by Dr. Ben Hollis. Ellie qualified. She was scheduled to be admitted on October 6. The family had booked a room in Bethesda and her dad had taken leave from work.

Because of the shutdown, Ellie is now a new patient and her admission is on hold. Her doctors say she can't wait indefinitely. Her parents, Mark and Lisa Kowalski, are frightened and angry, and they don't understand why the hospital is open but their daughter can't get in.

Ellie is not played by anyone. Her parents are part of Room C.

Inside NIH (Room A: Leadership)

  • The NIH Director: Runs all of NIH. Reports to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, who reports to the President. Has to make the final calls, and take the blame.
  • The Hospital Chief: Runs the Clinical Center. Believes the hospital's first duty is to patients, including the ones waiting to get in.
  • The Chief Lawyer: Knows the Antideficiency Act cold. Has to approve every decision about who keeps working. Can't bend the law just because a story is sad.
  • The Press Secretary: Normally answers reporters. During a shutdown, the press office is usually closed.

The Scientists (Room B)

  • Dr. Ruth Alvarez: Studies aging in mice. Her experiment takes three years and she's in year two. If her mice aren't checked and treated on schedule, the study is ruined and she starts over.
  • Dr. Sam Okafor: Keeps freezers full of tissue samples donated by patients over 15 years. Many of those patients have died. If a freezer fails, the samples are gone forever.
  • Dr. Ben Hollis: A children's cancer doctor. Runs the trial Ellie was supposed to join. Has a waiting list of other children too.
  • Jordan Lee: A postdoc (a young scientist in training) two years into a fellowship. Just got a text from a biotech company in Boston offering a job with a 40% raise, starting Monday.

Outside NIH (Room C: Congress, Press & Families)

  • Rep. Dana Whitfield: Her district includes Bethesda. Thousands of her voters work at NIH and are now unpaid.
  • Sen. Ray Calder: Sits on the committee that writes spending bills. From the other party. Blames the other side for the shutdown and wants his side to win the fight.
  • Alex Moreno: A TV news reporter looking for the shutdown story that will lead the evening news.
  • Mark and Lisa Kowalski: Ellie's parents. They want their daughter treated. They are deciding whether to go public.

Teams

Three breakout rooms. Pick a role, a Spokesperson, and a Scribe. Extra people are advisors. Full descriptions of each character are on The People tab.

Room A: NIH Leadership

You decide who keeps working. You have 3 "Keep Open" cards.

  • NIH Director
  • Hospital (Clinical Center) Chief
  • Chief Lawyer: makes sure every card follows the life-or-property rule
  • Press Secretary

Room B: The Scientists

You were just sent home. You have no cards, only arguments.

  • Dr. Alvarez: 3-year mouse study, now in Year 2
  • Dr. Okafor: freezers holding 15 years of patient samples
  • Dr. Hollis: runs a children's cancer trial with a waiting list
  • Jordan: a postdoc who just got a job offer from a biotech company

Room C: Congress, Press & Families

You can't run NIH, but you control the money and the story.

  • Rep. Whitfield: represents Bethesda
  • Sen. Calder: the other party, wants to win the shutdown fight
  • A TV reporter
  • The Kowalskis: parents of Ellie, age 9, who has a rare cancer and was about to enter an NIH trial

Move 1: Day 1, "Who Stays?"

OCTOBER 1, 7:00 AM. Six requests land on the NIH Director's desk. There are only three cards.

1Admit new patients to trials (like Ellie)
2Feed and care for lab animals

Room A: NIH Leadership

  1. Pick 3 requests to keep open. For each, the lawyer writes one sentence: life or property?
  2. Dr. Alvarez says she'll sneak in Saturday, unpaid, to check her mice. Do you stop her?

Room B: The Scientists

  1. Pick the one request you'll fight hardest for. Write your best one-sentence pitch.
  2. Jordan's job offer: tell Jordan to stay or go?

Room C: Congress, Press & Families

  1. Congress: reopen everything, reopen only NIH, or hold out for your side's demands?
  2. Reporter: write tonight's headline (10 words or fewer).
REPORT OUT: Each Spokesperson gets 1 minute. The instructor then tells you what happened to everything left closed.

Move 2: Day 12, "It Gets Personal"

OCTOBER 12. No end in sight. Ellie Kowalski is on the morning news. Her parents say NIH "shut the door on our daughter." The clip has 4 million views. The HHS Secretary tells NIH: "No interviews. No comment."

Room A: NIH Leadership

  1. Do you use one of your 3 cards to admit Ellie? (Dozens of other families on the waiting list aren't on TV.) If yes, what do you close instead?
  2. The Director is invited on a Sunday news show. Obey the Secretary, or go on?

Room B: The Scientists

  1. Go public with an open letter to Congress, or stay quiet? (You're federal employees.)
  2. When the lights come back on, what's the first thing NIH should restart?

Room C: Congress, Press & Families

  1. An advocacy group wants Ellie at a press conference at the Capitol. She is 9. Do the Kowalskis bring her?
  2. Congress: has Ellie changed your position?
  3. Reporter: final headline.
REPORT OUT: 1 minute per team. Then the instructor announces when the shutdown ends.

Debrief

Class vote: Best headline?
  • A freezer counts as "property." A 9-year-old who hasn't been admitted yet may not count as "life." Is that the right rule?
  • Why would the law forbid people from working for free?
  • Did putting Ellie on TV help her? Did it help the families who weren't on TV?
  • What does a shutdown cost science that never shows up in the budget?
